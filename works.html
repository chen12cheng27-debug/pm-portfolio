Salesforce CRM Integration

Consolidated three fragmented business models into a single Salesforce ecosystem — bridging Music, Audiobooks, and Podcasts into one unified operation.

Cross-Functional Alignment Hands-On Configuration Project Governance Timeline Protection

The Challenge

Three separate business units — Music, Audiobooks, and Podcasts — ran entirely different business models, worked through distinct undocumented workflows, and relied on isolated software tools. The management board issued a high-stakes directive: centralize all data and integrate these clashing processes into a single Salesforce CRM to unlock company-wide synergies.

Three siloed worlds, one system, zero room for losing what made each unit work.

DATA FLOW ARCHITECTURE
Conceptual architecture diagram — for illustrative purposes only

What I Did

Single point of contact for all three business units. I conducted deep process audits — not just collecting requirements, but sitting across from executives, team leads, and operators to hear what they were actually worried about, not only what they said in meetings.

Translated messy real-world realities into clear, structured specifications the external Salesforce consultants could act on. Three divisions, conflicting priorities, one voice.

Oversaw every dimension of execution: timelines, structured task assignments, agile ticketing, deliverable quality audits, budget tracking, and external invoice reviews.

The discipline was not in updating status reports — it was in knowing when a deadline needed defending and when a team needed breathing room. That is a call a spreadsheet never makes.

Budget was tight — we could not staff every technical task. So I shadowed the consultants, absorbed their methods, and upskilled rapidly on the platform itself.

I personally configured automated processes, custom workflows, system alerts, email integrations, and executed complex data migrations. By the project's midpoint, I was not just managing the build — I was inside the system, building it.

Shipping a system is easy. Getting people to use it is the real project. I designed a three-tier training framework:

  • Tier 1 — Company-wide kickoff presentation, framing Salesforce not as "new software to learn" but as a tool that makes daily work easier
  • Tier 2 — Three live, hands-on sessions, one per division, built around real cases from daily operations — not generic demos
  • Tier 3 — Advanced administrator training for nominated "Super Users" from each division, so day-to-day maintenance could be fully decentralized

The Impact

Unified Infrastructure — Collapsed three fragmented data environments into a single, high-leverage Salesforce ecosystem, delivering genuine data clarity to the management board.

Immediate Cost Savings — Saved significant consulting overhead by personally taking over backend configuration, automation building, and data migration.

Zero-Friction Adoption — Achieved total operational self-sufficiency across all three divisions; no consultancy dependency, no internal resistance, smooth transition to the new way of working.

Production Line Synergy

Aligned four geographically and culturally fragmented entities — US, Switzerland, Southeast Asia, and China — under one production line transfer project that had stalled completely.

Cross-Functional Translation Human Insight & Alignment High-Ambiguity Resilience

The Challenge

I joined a corporate integration project under Danaher: absorb the manufacturing capabilities of X-Rite, a newly acquired Swiss brand, and shift their operations to Videojet, the Southern China plant where I was based.

On paper, it was a straightforward cost-saving move. In practice, four parties were pulling in four different directions. Corporate leadership in the US wanted speed. The acquired management team in Switzerland was skeptical and protective. Tier-1 suppliers in Southeast Asia were uncooperative. And our own plant in China was already running at capacity, with no appetite for extra load.

The time zones made it worse. There was no window where everyone could meet. The project stalled.

STAKEHOLDER ALIGNMENT FLOW
Conceptual architecture diagram — for illustrative purposes only

What I Did

I could feel the tension within days of joining. The US team ran on process and structure. The Swiss team worked freestyle. Neither side understood the other, and standard reporting channels were useless. So I became the filter: raw updates came in from all four regions, I stripped out the emotional noise, and I sent back a clean version that focused on what each side was actually doing to move things forward.

I listened to everyone, and I mean actually listened. The China plant had real fears about capacity overload. Switzerland had real anxieties about being absorbed. I didn't take sides or let regional biases color my judgment. Over time, that neutrality built enough trust that people started dropping their guard.

I didn't just forward emails. When a bottleneck hit, like a supplier delay in Southeast Asia, I dug into the data myself. Then I came back to the group not with bad news, but with options: here are three things we can do, here's what each one costs, here's my recommendation. The conversation shifted from "whose fault is this" to "which option do we pick."

The Impact

Global Information Funnel — Became the single trusted source of updates across all four regions. People stopped guessing what the other side was doing.

Cultural Bridge — Earned enough trust from all sides that hostility between US, Swiss, and Chinese teams gave way to actual collaboration.

Trust Across All Sides — The production line transferred successfully. What had been a deadlocked project became a working operation.

Digital Transformation

Diagnosed operational reality across an entire company through 33 deep-dive audits — then turned the findings into the blueprint for a company-wide transformation.

Upstream Strategic Diagnostics High-Ambiguity Resilience Executive Buy-In Process Architecture

The Challenge

When I joined Zebralution, requests came in from every business division — each wanting fixes for their own systems and workflows. I ran localized Kaizen workshops and targeted upgrades, but it was clear that incremental patches weren't enough. The company needed a structural overhaul.

A new COO was coming in, hungry for change. I didn't wait for instructions. I built a case for a company-wide digital transformation, pitched it to the executive board, and secured the mandate to lead phase one.

What I Did

My colleague and I designed a structured discovery phase. We sent targeted questionnaires a week ahead, then ran 33 intensive interviews with core roles across every division. But interviews only get you so far. I also embedded myself into the daily work of key roles — shadowing people for days, watching how they actually used their tools, where they got stuck, what they'd stopped mentioning because nothing ever changed.

I took 33 messy human narratives and turned them into structured process documentation. For the first time at Zebralution, someone could look at one document and trace how their work connected to the person sitting next to them — and the person three departments over. That common ground didn't exist before.

I categorized hundreds of findings into a 2×3 matrix. First split: Hard Issues (broken technology, legacy systems) vs. Soft Issues (process friction, communication gaps). Then mapped across three levels: company-wide structural problems, cross-divisional friction, and division-specific bottlenecks. What had been an endless list of complaints became a prioritization tool.

I ran regular sessions with the C-suite. High-level progress, validated findings, clear decision points. No trivia. The goal was to maintain their confidence and keep the mandate moving into design and implementation.

33 Deep-Dive Audits — Sat down with key roles across every division and pulled out the real, unfiltered picture of how things actually worked.

Architectural Matrix — Sorted hundreds of pain points into a clean Hard vs. Soft matrix, so executives could see where to invest and what to fix first.

B2B Digital Asset Architecture

Designed and launched id.entities, an identity verification tool, from concept to version 3.5 — solving an industry-wide royalty crisis that streaming platforms refused to fix.

End-to-End Product Ownership B2B Client Co-Creation Agile Tooling Matrix Multilingual GTM Strategy

The Challenge

Labels and artists kept reporting royalty calculation discrepancies. Tracing the data back through the supply chain, we found the root cause: streaming platforms were withholding payouts because their systems couldn't tell apart artists with the same name. The platforms were blunt. "If you, as the distributor, cannot solve this identity mapping problem, we cannot help you."

It was a platform-side failure, but the friction was landing on us. I took ownership of the problem and started designing a B2B product from scratch — anchored by id.entities.

What I Did

I brought engineering, content operations, and our most affected labels into the same room. Through structured workshops with the clients who originally flagged the crisis, I translated their frustration into concrete product features.

I ran iterative user testing groups with core labels throughout the build, from early wireframes to production deployment. This kept the backend aligned with how people actually worked day to day.

I built the training ecosystem: English and German video tutorials, user flow charts, localized FAQs. Then I trained internal Label Managers on every feature, walking them through likely client objections so they could upsell confidently.

I set up a framework for collecting feature requests and usage data after the initial release. By feeding user insights back into development, I pushed id.entities through 3.5 generations of live iterations before I left.

The Impact

0-to-1 Commercial Solution — Conceived, defined, and launched id.entities with batch approvals, auto-matching, auto-alerts, etc.

Lifecycle Evolution (v3.5) — A continuous feedback loop drove the tool through 3.5 versions of live iteration.

Multilingual GTM Coverage — Authored the full English and German content ecosystem, eliminating onboarding friction and enabling Account Managers to upsell independently.

Small Winery Project

Designed and executed a pre-sell business model connecting Chinese high-end wine buyers with exclusive French wineries — then validated the model again in an MBA entrepreneurship competition, winning first prize.

0-to-1 Business Design Commercial Execution Client Insight Pre-Sell Model High-Ticket Sales Team Mobilization

The Challenge

At Nouvell Sante, I spent a lot of time at wine events talking with our clients. A pattern started to show: our high-end customers cared less about price and more about two things. Exclusivity — wines not available on the mass market. And heritage — the story and prestige of the winery behind the bottle.

The founders saw an opportunity. The company's other founder was a French sommelier with deep connections in the French wine industry. He could reach small, prestigious wineries in France — the kind producing under 30,000 bottles a year, with rich history, selling only top-tier wines, and crucially, not yet open to the Chinese market.

The model was pre-sell. We would secure a winery partner, present their wines to our selected clients, take orders and payment upfront, then use that capital to import and deliver. No inventory risk. End-to-end responsibility from sourcing to delivery. For bulk buyers, we also offered warehousing and consignment.

PRE-SELL BUSINESS MODEL

Conceptual architecture diagram — for illustrative purposes only

What I Did

I took over the fundraising side of the project. First, I worked with key account managers to identify and invite our most relevant fine-wine clients to an exclusive event. I prepared the business presentation: the winery's history, the deal terms, and why this wine mattered. We set up POS terminals so clients could pay on the spot. At the event, I delivered the pitch in Chinese and translated for the French founder. I also helped close several contracts. The combination of a well-prepared room, a clear deal, and motivated staff on the floor worked. We hit the fundraising target.

Before the event, we restructured operations for the campaign. Every employee, regardless of department, was trained to close contracts. We built a transparent commission structure where each verified contract triggered an instant bonus. That company-wide alignment is what made the target achievable — when 30 people are rowing in the same direction, the math works.

Later, during my MBA, I assembled a five-person team and entered the entrepreneurship competition with the same business model. This time the product was German ice wine — even tighter supply — and the target was Chinese women with strong purchasing power and an eye for quality. The model held. The team actually sold a batch of ice wine to the target audience during the competition. We won first prize, and the angel investors on the judging panel validated the approach.

The Impact

Fundraising target met — The event successfully pre-sold the first batch of exclusive French wine to high-end clients, covering import and delivery costs upfront.

Full-cycle execution — From client insight to supplier deal to fundraising event to delivery. A complete 0-to-1 commercial project.

MBA validation — The same model, applied to ice wine and a different customer segment, won first prize in the school's entrepreneurship competition with angel investor endorsement.
